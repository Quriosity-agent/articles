The map groups 63 highly valued AI “neolabs” that are mostly pre-product-market-fit and sub-revenue-scale, but are capitalized like future platform companies. Their shared pattern is not simply “building another LLM”; each is trying to move AI research into a more specific control point: robotics, world models, scientific discovery, mathematics, inference infrastructure, enterprise agents, voice, video, or brain-computer interfaces.

Below is a one-paragraph description of what each company appears to be doing, in the same order as the chart. Many are still stealth or only partially public, so these summaries are grounded in the chart’s founders / known-for / category fields plus public positioning where available.

## One paragraph per company

### 01. Roze (Robotics)

A SoftBank-led robotics-AI initiative from Masayoshi Son, aiming less at another chatbot and more at recombining large models, embodied intelligence, and SoftBank’s robotics assets for the physical world.

### 02. Thinking Machines Lab (Frontier lab)

A frontier-model lab founded by ex-OpenAI and PyTorch figures including Mira Murati, John Schulman, and Soumith Chintala, focused on new human-model interaction patterns as much as raw model scale.

### 03. Project Prometheus (Robotics)

A Jeff Bezos-backed AI operating company pointed at the physical economy: moving AI from software assistance into manufacturing, warehousing, logistics, and robotic execution.

### 04. SSI (Safe Superintelligence) (Frontier lab)

Ilya Sutskever’s safe-superintelligence lab, whose “product” is not a near-term API but a long-horizon research program for safely training frontier AGI/ASI systems.

### 05. Reflection AI (Frontier lab / Open source)

Founded by ex-DeepMind researchers, Reflection AI is trying to build U.S.-based open frontier models, balancing open weights, reasoning capability, and enterprise controllability.

### 06. Skild AI (Robotics)

A robotics-foundation-model company from CMU professors Deepak Pathak and Abhinav Gupta, aiming to let many robot bodies share perception, control, and manipulation skills instead of hand-coding each machine.

### 07. Poolside (Frontier lab / Coding)

A coding-model company from the GitHub Copilot lineage, training software-engineering-specific models and developer environments rather than simply plugging generic chat models into IDEs.

### 08. Physical Intelligence (Robotics)

A general robot-intelligence company from Stanford/Google robotics researchers, training transferable physical skills from web-scale data, simulation, and real-world manipulation.

### 09. Periodic Labs (Bio / Materials)

A materials-science AI lab involving Liam Fedus and Ekin Dogus Cubuk, using models to accelerate materials discovery by turning experimentation into a plan-generate-verify loop.

### 10. Ineffable Intelligence (Frontier lab)

David Silver’s new lab, likely centered on reinforcement learning, search, and general reasoning—an extension of the DeepMind playbook of solving hard problems before packaging products.

### 11. World Labs (World models)

Fei-Fei Li’s spatial-intelligence company building world models so AI can understand 3D scenes, object relations, and actionable spatial structure rather than merely labeling images.

### 12. Recursive Superintelligence (Frontier lab / Continual)

Founded by Richard Socher, Tim Rocktäschel, Josh Tobin, and Jeff Clune, this lab is about self-improving AI: continual learning, tool use, and automated research loops that upgrade the system itself.

### 13. AMI Labs (World models)

A European world-model company associated with Yann LeCun and Alexandre LeBrun, likely extending LeCun’s JEPA/predictive-learning view of non-autoregressive world modeling.

### 14. Unconventional AI (Compute)

Naveen Rao’s post-MosaicML company, focused less on an application and more on redesigning the compute, chip, or systems stack behind AI training and inference.

### 15. Humans& (Frontier lab)

A research-first lab from Eric Zelikman and Georges Harik, combining xAI-style research with early Google organizational experience to explore stronger reasoning, synthetic data, and autonomous research systems.

### 16. Core Automation (Frontier lab / Continual)

Former OpenAI VP Jerry Tworek’s “most automated AI lab,” focused on automating research, experiments, evaluation, and training workflows so the lab itself becomes a high-throughput machine.

### 17. Inflection AI (Enterprise AI)

An enterprise-AI company founded by Mustafa Suleyman, Karen Simonyan, and Reid Hoffman; after Pi, much of the team and technology shifted toward Microsoft, leaving an enterprise-assistant/model-service trajectory.

### 18. Hark (Enterprise AI)

A Brett Adcock-funded enterprise-AI effort, likely combining lessons from robotics, aviation, and business automation into AI systems for company workflows.

### 19. Recursive (Hardware / Chip design)

Founded by Anna Goldie and Azalia Mirhoseini, Recursive applies AI to chip design—automating layout, architecture search, and optimization so semiconductor R&D itself is accelerated.

### 20. Xaira Therapeutics (Bio / Drug discovery)

## What the map really says

First, capital is splitting the “AI lab” template into many vertical research companies: robotics, materials, drug discovery, math, video, voice, chip design, simulation, and inference are all absorbing talent from the major labs. Second, these valuations are not justified by current revenue; they are bets on who will control a new platform layer if a foundational capability breaks through. Third, the key question is not which company has the best demo today, but which control surface it owns: data, compute, hardware, architecture, evaluation, lab automation, or distribution.

In one sentence: these are not 63 chatbot startups; they are 63 bets on where the next AI platform layer will land—physical reality, scientific experimentation, software engineering, inference infrastructure, or human-computer interfaces.
