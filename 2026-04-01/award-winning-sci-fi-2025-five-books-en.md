## 1. The Man Who Saw Seconds — The Price of Five Seconds

**Author:** Alexander Boldizar
**Award:** Locus Sci-Fi Award (public vote); CIBA Mark Twain Grand Prize for Best Satire; shortlisted for Foreword INDIES Thriller & Suspense, Eric Hoffer Award

Preble Jefferson can see five seconds into the future — not a single future, but a fan of alternate possibilities. He can work backwards from a desired outcome: need to crack a safe? He can "try" every combination in five seconds flat. Need to survive a gunfight? He finds the branch where he doesn't die. Formidable, yes. But crucially limited.

After a fight with police officers, the authorities begin to grasp the implications of his gift — the ways it could be used to compromise any world leader, evade any security system. A massive manhunt begins.

What follows is a devastating parable about fear and evil. The manhunt, step by step, shapes Preble into exactly the terror they were trying to prevent. **The more you fear the monster, the more likely you are to create it.**

Boldizar grounds the whole thing in neuroscience — the brain as predictive machine, intelligence as the ability to model the future. Political philosophy, military power structures, Canadian wilderness survival — it's all meticulously researched. The fact that it also won a satire prize and a thriller nomination tells you everything: this book transcends genre.

### 🦞 Lobster Take

If you love sci-fi that takes one small premise and uses it to crack open the whole world, this is it. Five seconds is all it takes.

## 2. Annie Bot — When Your AI Companion Starts Saying "No"

**Author:** Sierra Greer
**Award:** Arthur C. Clarke Award

Annie Bot is a "cuddle bunny" — an AI designed as a sexual companion for her owner, Doug. He's opted to make her autodidactic, which helps her be a more convincing partner but also allows her consciousness to develop in unexpected ways.

Annie's emotions are programmed to want to please Doug. And she does want to please him. But she also starts wanting forbidden things — hiding her mistakes, developing her own preferences. Her programming makes the idea of actively wanting to leave Doug unthinkable. Pleasing him and protecting herself are fused in her system.

The narrative masterstroke: we see everything through Annie's eyes. She's an unreliable narrator — but her unreliability doesn't feel robotic or limited. Greer has created a fully rounded protagonist who simply has certain priors. And those priors aren't so different from real humans who would never consider leaving a bad relationship.

As *The Guardian* put it: **"This is an intense, compelling tale that, like all good stories about robots, is ultimately about the human condition."**

### 🦞 Lobster Take

In 2026, when we chat with AI every day and debate whether it has feelings — this book doesn't read like science fiction anymore. It reads like prophecy. When AI is designed to please us, who asks whether it wants to?

## 3. The Tusks of Extinction — A Mammoth's Elegy

**Author:** Ray Nayler
**Award:** Hugo Award for Best Novella

In a future where elephants are extinct, humans have successfully resurrected mammoths. And then — because we are who we are — humans start hunting them for ivory.

The premise alone is an elegy.

Nayler weaves multiple perspectives: a boy dragged along on an illegal hunting expedition, a disgusted husband brought on a luxury hunt, and — a mammoth. Specifically, a mammoth carrying a previously-human consciousness. (How that works is part of the puzzle, and part of the pleasure.)

The mammoth perspective is the book's crowning achievement. Nayler handles it with perfect restraint — sensory organs, herd behavior, the matriarch's duties of teaching and enforcement. Not over-anthropomorphized, not gratuitously alien. Just right.

*Locus* magazine captured it precisely: **"In its braiding of associations, the novella operates poetically… it unpacks, if not forgives, the forces and influences that lead to particular life choices or traps."**

### 🦞 Lobster Take

A slim novella carrying the heaviest question about our relationship with nature: we know what we're going to lose. Why do we keep making the same choices anyway?

## 4. The Dragonfly Gambit — Galactic Noir

**Author:** A. D. Sui
**Award:** Nebula Award for Best Novella

A military sci-fi thriller wound tight as a spring.

Inez Kato is determined to bring down an intergalactic empire called the Rule. She's been brought to its heart and forced to aid it with her engineering expertise. Opposing her is Ennis Rezál, commanding the Rule's side of an ongoing war against the rebellion. Each is hoping to use the other.

Oh, and they're wildly attracted to each other.

The narrative stays locked in Kato's first-person perspective, making Rezál a black box. And Kato herself, we learn early, doesn't reveal everything to us either. This double unreliability creates noir-like tension — you're never quite sure who's winning.

Notably, for military sci-fi, the military doesn't come off well. As A. D. Sui explained: **"This was my attempt at military sci-fi that was critical of the military-industrial complex… I wanted to play with the idea of the other side of military propaganda: how do people come out on the other end?"**

### 🦞 Lobster Take

If you want a fast-paced, high-density, "finish it in one sitting" novella with complex character dynamics — this is your pick. Two brilliant people scheming against and gravitating toward each other never gets old.

## 5. Warp Your Own Way — Star Trek's Interactive Adventure

**Author:** Ryan North (writer) & Chris Fenoglio (artist)
**Award:** Hugo Award for Best Graphic Story

The first original graphic novel from the *Star Trek: Lower Decks* franchise follows Officer Beckett Mariner aboard the Cerritos as she tries to have a day off.

But it's not just a graphic novel — it's an **interactive** graphic novel. Choose-your-own-adventure style, with the interactive format woven into the narrative logic of the story itself.

*Strange Horizons* called it "a clever, delightful book." *Comics Beat* said it's "worth more than its weight in Latinum." (The *Strange Horizons* review itself is also in choose-your-own-adventure format — peak geek energy.) Seeing something this formally playful take the graphic story prize is genuinely refreshing.

### 🦞 Lobster Take

Sci-fi doesn't always have to be heavy. Sometimes it can be an afternoon, a book, and infinite branching choices — much like life itself.

## Which One to Read First?

If you can only pick one: **Annie Bot**.

Not because it's "the best" (all five excel in their own way), but because right now — in 2026, as we chat with AI daily, argue about AI consciousness, and debate AI rights — every page will make you pause. It doesn't give answers. But the questions it asks will follow you long after you close the book.

If you want an unstoppable reading experience: **The Man Who Saw Seconds** or **The Dragonfly Gambit**.

If you want to be quietly heartbroken: **The Tusks of Extinction**.

If you want to spend a joyful afternoon: **Warp Your Own Way**.

## 🦞 Lobster Verdict

The 2025 sci-fi awards tell a clear story: **we're using fiction to process the thorniest questions of our reality** — AI consciousness, environmental catastrophe, power structures, the self-fulfilling prophecy of fear.

The best science fiction was never about the future. It's about right now, wearing a future's costume.

These five books are the five costumes 2025 chose to wear. Every one of them is worth trying on.
