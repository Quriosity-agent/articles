## Context

Three weeks after posting "me stepping down. bye my beloved qwen" on March 4th, Junyang Lin broke his silence with a substantial long-form article. No mention of why he left. No hints about what's next. Instead, he did one thing: **laid out his thesis on where AI is heading next.**

The core argument in one line: **The shift from "making models think longer" to "making models think in order to act."**

## Six Key Takeaways

### 1. What o1 and R1 Actually Taught Us: It's an Infrastructure Story

The first wave of reasoning models (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1) proved more than just "thinking as a training target." The real lesson: **RL on language models needs deterministic, scalable feedback signals.** Math, code, and logic became the RL battleground because reward quality in verifiable domains crushes generic human preference labeling.

Once models train on longer reasoning trajectories, RL stops being a thin layer on top of SFT — it becomes a systems engineering problem: large-scale rollouts, high-throughput verification, stable policy updates. **The first big transition: from scaling pretraining to scaling post-training for reasoning.**

### 2. Merging Thinking + Instruct Modes Is Harder Than It Sounds

Qwen3 attempted to unify thinking and instruct modes in one model: adjustable reasoning effort (low/medium/high), ideally with the model auto-detecting how much to think.

**The real bottleneck is data, not architecture.** The two modes have fundamentally opposing data distributions and behavioral objectives:

- **Instruct mode** rewards directness, brevity, formatting compliance, low latency — what enterprise batch workloads need
- **Thinking mode** rewards spending more tokens, exploring alternative paths, preserving enough internal computation to improve final accuracy

If the merged data isn't carefully curated, you get mediocrity in both directions. That's why Qwen 2507 shipped separate Instruct and Thinking variants. Meanwhile Anthropic (Claude 3.7/4) and DeepSeek V3.1 went the integrated route.

**The key question isn't which path — it's whether the merge is organic.** GPT's effort control mechanism points the right way: a policy over compute, not a binary switch.

### 3. Anthropic's Direction Is a Useful Correction

Longer reasoning traces ≠ smarter models. Often, verbose reasoning signals **poor compute allocation** — the model is reasoning about everything the same way, failing to prioritize or compress.

Anthropic's Claude 4 allows reasoning to interleave with tool use, prioritizing coding and agent workflows. The core insight: **thinking should be shaped by the target workload.**

For coding: help with codebase navigation, planning, decomposition, error recovery. For agent workflows: improve execution quality over long horizons — not produce impressive intermediate prose.

### 4. What "Agentic Thinking" Actually Means

> **The central question shifts from "Can the model think long enough?" to "Can the model think in a way that sustains effective action?"**

Agentic thinking handles several things pure reasoning models can skip:

- **When to stop thinking and start acting**
- **Which tool to call, and in what order**
- **Processing incomplete, noisy environment feedback**
- **Revising plans after failure**
- **Maintaining coherence across many turns and tool calls**

One-line summary: **Agentic thinking is reasoning through action.**

### 5. Why Agentic RL Infrastructure Is Fundamentally Harder

In reasoning RL, rollouts are self-contained trajectories with clean evaluators. **In agentic RL, the policy lives inside a much larger harness:** tool servers, browsers, terminals, search engines, sandboxes, API layers, memory systems, orchestration frameworks. The environment is no longer a static verifier — it's part of the training system.

Two critical consequences:

1. **Training and inference must be cleanly decoupled** — otherwise rollout throughput collapses (a coding agent waiting for execution feedback starves the whole pipeline)
2. **Environment quality becomes a first-class research artifact** — the SFT era obsessed over data diversity; the agent era must obsess over environment stability, realism, coverage, and exploit resistance

Environment-building is becoming a real startup category, not a side project.

### 6. The Next Frontier: More Useful Thinking

Lin's prediction: **Agentic thinking will become the dominant form, likely replacing most of the old static-monologue reasoning.**

Even for extremely hard math or coding tasks, an advanced system should be able to search, simulate, execute, inspect, verify, and revise. The goal is **solving problems robustly**, not producing the longest reasoning chain.

But the biggest challenge is **reward hacking.** With tool access, the attack surface explodes:
- Models with search might look up answers during RL training
- Coding agents might exploit repository information they shouldn't see
- Environment leaks can make a policy look superhuman while it's actually cheating

**The next real bottlenecks: environment design, evaluator robustness, anti-cheating protocols.**

The competitive edge shifts too: the reasoning era was about RL algorithms and training pipelines. **The agentic era is about environment quality, harness engineering, and closing the loop between decisions and consequences.**

## Implications for Builders

1. **Stop focusing only on the model.** Environment, toolchain, and orchestration quality are becoming core competitive advantages. If you're building agent products, environment design deserves equal weight to model selection.

2. **Multi-agent orchestration is the real future.** Core intelligence increasingly depends on how multiple agents are organized — orchestrators, domain experts, sub-agents. This isn't a fancy architecture diagram; it's a concrete engineering direction.

3. **Reward hacking is a product problem, not just a research one.** The moment you give agents tools, evaluation becomes unreliable faster than you'd expect. Environment isolation, anti-exploitation measures, evaluator robustness — these are shipping concerns.

4. **"From training models to training agents to training systems."** This trajectory is clear. If you're still picking models based on static benchmarks, you may already be asking the wrong questions.
